Five verdicts, never a bluff

It knows the difference between wrong, and don't-know.

Most systems have two states: an answer, or nothing. Weryta has five — because "the evidence conflicts" is not "there is no evidence", and an association is not yet a claim.

Entailed

The record directly supports it — with the source and the dates it held.

Contradicted

The record directly says the opposite — a withdrawal, a supersession.

Ambiguous

The corpus holds the claim and its negation at equal specificity. Not missing knowledge — a conflict in the documents that a human has to settle.

Associated

Nothing is claimed. The associative layer links the concepts and points at where to look — a retrieval suggestion, never an answer.

Unknown

No basis in the documents you gave it. It refuses to invent one.

"Derived" is not a verdict — it is a kind of answer inside a proof pack: an inference from parent facts, shown with its full chain and rule, never as a stored fact.

Tasks: single supporting fact, time reasoning, deduction by inheritance. For scale, an untrained LSTM scores roughly 50/27/21% here. Separately, on an internal range of worlds generated from hidden semantics that candidate rules never see, one gate is absolute: zero false assertions — a rule that states an untruth even once does not pass, no matter how much it gains elsewhere.

Weryta would rather say Unknown than guess — and we have a number for that, not a slogan.

Decision ledger

A history that cannot be quietly rewritten.

Every decision to accept or reject a fact is appended to a ledger in which each row seals the previous one with a cryptographic hash. Cut a single row, or change a single character, and the chain no longer adds up — visibly, immediately. A mistake is never fixed by overwriting: it is a new row.

The head of that chain is anchored into Bitcoin via OpenTimestamps. We store no data there — only the hash is anchored. Thirty-two bytes leave the building. Your documents never do.

Rewriting our history would mean forging a Bitcoin block. Verification runs offline, against your own node — you do not have to trust us to check it.

A second witness

Two models agreeing is not proof.

A fact about when a rule came into force is accepted automatically only when an independent state registry confirms it (the Polish parliament's ELI interface). A model can be confidently wrong; a registry is a witness that does not guess.

From our own ledger: the model gave an effective date of 2020-01-02, the registry answered 2020-01-06. The fact never entered the store. Two models agreeing is not proof. Agreeing with the state registry is.

Two axes of time

What held then — and what was known then.

Weryta separates valid time (when a rule was actually in force) from knowledge time (when we learned of it). That is what lets it answer the question audits actually ask: "what was true on 1 March 2021, as far as anyone knew on 1 June 2021" — rather than only "what is true today".

Two axes of time are decades-old computer science (bitemporality, SQL:2011) and we did not invent them. What Weryta adds is a proof pack you can verify offline, without trusting us.

What you need to run it

An ordinary laptop. No GPU. No cloud.

Weryta is a small Rust binary that runs offline on hardware you already own. Checking a proof pack needs no graphics card and no network either.

Heavier hardware is required only if you want to run local language models in the learning pipeline — Weryta itself, and proof verification, do not.

How it learns

Asking often does not make an answer truer.

The system learns by growing the set of verified facts — not by tuning weights on the path to an answer. The associative layer helps find the right documents; what gets asserted is decided by evidence alone.

The channel through which frequent use could have quietly flipped a verdict was measured, judged dangerous, and closed at the compiler level — by types, not by policy: the field is private and read-only, so code that could rewrite truth through usage simply does not compile.
